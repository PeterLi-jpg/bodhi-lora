\begin{abstract}
How should we evaluate and instill the behaviors that distinguish calibrated expert reasoning from confident answer generation? Clarifying inquiry, scope bounding, principled abstention, and recognition of clinical warning signs are central to safe deployment in high-stakes domains, yet remain poorly measured and rarely targeted by current training pipelines. We contribute a framework for studying these epistemic behaviors as first-class objects of evaluation and design, and apply it to open-source clinical language models. The framework has three components. First, a seven-dimension behavioral decomposition (uncertainty acknowledgment, active inquiry, context-seeking, red-flag identification, scope bounding, hedging quality, specificity) that separates \emph{how} a model communicates from \emph{what} it knows, enabling claims about behavior that aggregate clinical scores obscure. Second, an asymmetric cross-family grading protocol (Qwen-14B as quality filter, Llama-8B as evaluator) that prevents filter-grader bias from inflating reported metrics, addressing a known failure mode in self-distillation pipelines. Third, a four-step pipeline for teaching epistemic behaviors to open-source models: a structured calibration reasoning protocol generates demonstrations, expert rubrics filter quality cases, and a LoRA adapter internalizes the behavior in the weights. Applied to MedGemma-27B on HealthBench with ${\sim}$3,100 filtered traces per seed, the resulting open-weight adapter raises active clinical inquiry from 17.5\% to 45.6\% ($d{=}0.63$, $n{\approx}1{,}000$), context-seeking from 1.40 to 1.75 on a 0--2 scale ($d{=}0.44$), red-flag identification from 1.52 to 1.67 ($d{=}0.20$), and scope bounding from 1.91 to 1.97 ($d{=}0.22$), with aggregate clinical quality preserved ($p{=}0.57$) and blanket disclaimers eliminated. We additionally surface a failure mode worth studying in its own right: combining the inference-time reasoning protocol with the adapted model degrades red-flag identification below the unadapted baseline, indicating that calibrated behavior interacts non-additively with inference-time scaffolding in ways current evaluation practices would miss. We release code, adapters, and the evaluation framework to support work on humble, uncertain, and inquiring open-source models.
\end{abstract}

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} clinical language models; calibration; behavioral evaluation; LoRA fine-tuning; chain-of-thought distillation; epistemic uncertainty; active inquiry; medical AI safety; HealthBench; self-distillation.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
%======================================================================

A clinical language model that scores well on multiple-choice benchmarks is not the same as one that exhibits the behaviors of a careful expert. Careful experts ask before answering, hedge when uncertain, name danger signs, and decline when they need more context. These behaviors---active inquiry, scope bounding, red-flag recognition, principled abstention---distinguish a calibrated expert from a confident guesser. They are also precisely what current training pipelines suppress, what current benchmarks fail to measure, and what we lack systematic methods to instill.

The problem has two parts: measurement and training. On measurement: aggregate clinical quality scores conflate \emph{what} a model knows with \emph{how} it communicates. A model can produce a clinically accurate response while omitting relevant uncertainty, failing to ask for missing context, or missing a warning sign that should trigger escalation; aggregate scores treat such a response as equivalent to a genuinely calibrated one. On training: language models trained with RLHF exhibit a systematic bias toward confident outputs \citep{kadavath2022language, lin2022teaching}. Human raters in preference labeling favor decisive responses over qualified ones \citep{xiong2024can}, and standard benchmarks reinforce this bias by scoring hedging and abstention as failures. The behaviors we want to encourage are penalized by the very objectives used to train and evaluate.

We use the term \emph{epistemic virtues} to refer to the behaviors that characterize calibrated expert reasoning: uncertainty acknowledgment, clarifying question-asking, scope bounding, evidence qualification, and principled abstention. Medical education has long recognized that expert judgment requires tolerating rather than eliminating uncertainty \citep{simpkin2016tolerating}; safety-netting---naming danger signs and when to return---is a canonical clinical skill \citep{jones2019safety}. Current clinical LLMs suppress both, training away from qualified responses toward confident ones \citep{ouyang2022training, wornow2023shaky}. Building these behaviors into clinical models requires tools that can measure them precisely and training approaches that can target them directly.

Our starting point is the \bodhi{} protocol (\textbf{B}ridging, \textbf{O}pen, \textbf{D}iscerning, \textbf{H}umble, \textbf{I}nquiring) \citep{bodhi2026}, a structured chain-of-thought (CoT) protocol that forces a model through multi-step uncertainty analysis before generating a response. Pass~1 generates an internal analysis (clinical assessment, differentials, evidence quality, key uncertainties, questions to ask, scope boundaries, safe recommendations); Pass~2 generates a patient-facing response conditioned on that analysis. The CoT protocol reliably elicits the target epistemic behaviors at inference time, at $\sim$2$\times$ inference cost and with a $\sim$5\% response drop rate (context window exceeded). This makes it a useful demonstration source: run it on a large prompt set and you have a corpus of calibrated behavior at scale. It also makes it a poor deployment solution on its own---remove the CoT protocol and the model reverts.

This paper contributes a framework for studying epistemic behaviors as first-class objects of evaluation and design, and uses LoRA fine-tuning on CoT-generated demonstrations as evidence that the framework's behavioral dimensions are real, measurable, and respond to design interventions. The framework has two components: (1) a seven-dimension behavioral decomposition that separates \emph{how} a model communicates from \emph{what} it knows, paired with an asymmetric cross-family grading protocol (Qwen-14B filter, Llama-8B evaluator) that prevents self-distillation bias from inflating reported metrics; and (2) a recipe for teaching epistemic behaviors via CoT-conditioned demonstrations. Applied to MedGemma-27B \citep{medgemma2025} on 4,000 HealthBench \citep{healthbench2025} prompts with 5 independent seeds, the recipe produces LoRA adapters that shift multiple behavioral dimensions significantly without degrading clinical quality. The framework additionally surfaces a design constraint invisible to aggregate evaluation: inference-time and weight-based epistemic behaviors compete rather than compose, with consequences for how adapted clinical models should be deployed.

Our contributions:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{A design constraint with safety implications}: stacking the \bodhi{} CoT protocol on the LoRA-adapted model degrades red-flag identification below the unadapted baseline ($d=-0.25$, $p<10^{-7}$), even as active inquiry rises to 80\%. CoT-induced and weight-based epistemic behaviors compete for the same behavioral capacity---a finding invisible to aggregate evaluation and directly relevant to deployment decisions for adapted clinical models.
    \item An \textbf{epistemic virtue evaluation framework}: a seven-dimension behavioral decomposition (uncertainty acknowledgment, active inquiry, context-seeking, red-flag identification, scope bounding, hedging quality, specificity) that separates \emph{how} a model communicates from \emph{what} it knows, enabling the above finding and other behavioral claims that aggregate clinical scores obscure. The framework includes an asymmetric cross-family grading protocol (Qwen-14B as quality filter, Llama-8B as evaluator) that prevents filter-grader bias---a known failure mode in self-distillation pipelines.
    \item \textbf{Evidence that the framework's behavioral dimensions respond to design, and an internalization result}: quality-filtered SFT on \bodhi{} CoT demonstrations achieves active inquiry $d=0.63$, context-seeking $d=0.44$, scope bounding $d=0.22$, red-flag identification $d=0.20$, all $p<10^{-5}$, $n{\approx}1{,}000$ per condition, with no degradation in aggregate clinical quality ($d=0.03$, $p=0.57$) and blanket disclaimers eliminated. The bootstrap 95\% CI for the LoRA${-}$Wrapper active-inquiry difference is $[-0.069, +0.021]$ (contains zero): \textbf{the adapter is statistically indistinguishable from the wrapper while requiring no inference-time CoT, delivering wrapper-equivalent behavior at ${\sim}58$\% lower inference output cost}.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
%======================================================================

\paragraph{Calibration in language models.} \citet{kadavath2022language} demonstrated that LLMs possess internal uncertainty representations accessible via probing, but that RLHF-tuned outputs suppress this uncertainty. Instruction tuning with human feedback \citep{ouyang2022training} systematically reinforces this suppression: human raters in preference labeling favor decisive responses over qualified ones \citep{xiong2024can}, so the alignment objective actively penalizes appropriate hedging. \citet{lin2022teaching} fine-tuned models on demonstrations with verbalized confidence (``I'm about 70\% sure...''), improving calibration on factual questions. \citet{tian2023just} showed similar effects from prompting alone. These works target \emph{numerical} confidence calibration. Our work targets \emph{behavioral} calibration: we do not ask the model to estimate its confidence as a number, but to modify its response behavior---hedging, questioning, abstaining---in ways appropriate to its uncertainty. The distinction matters clinically: a model can achieve low ECE on a calibration curve while still omitting the key safety-netting questions that a practitioner would ask.

\paragraph{Clinical epistemic behaviors.} Medicine has long recognized that expert judgment requires tolerating uncertainty rather than eliminating it. \citet{simpkin2016tolerating} argue that medical education must train clinicians to act appropriately under irreducible uncertainty rather than seeking false certainty---a principle directly reflected in our behavioral decomposition. Safety-netting---telling patients what to watch for and when to return \citep{jones2019safety}---is a canonical clinical behavior that our red-flag identification and scope-bounding dimensions operationalize. \citet{wornow2023shaky} document that current clinical LLMs trained on EHR data inherit the overconfident patterns of clinical notes rather than the calibrated uncertainty communication of a clinician explaining to a patient. Our work addresses this gap directly.

\paragraph{Medical LLM evaluation.} Standard medical benchmarks (MedQA, MMLU-Medical) evaluate factual accuracy via multiple-choice questions. HealthBench \citep{healthbench2025} differs by evaluating open-ended clinical responses against expert rubrics with both positive criteria (correct content, appropriate referrals) and negative criteria (dangerous advice, false reassurance, failure to escalate). This structure penalizes both overconfidence (meeting negative criteria) and excessive hedging (failing positive criteria for actionable guidance), making it suitable for evaluating calibrated behavior. We adopt the HealthBench grading protocol and extend it with prompt-injection hardening and cross-family grading.

\paragraph{Inference-time behavioral interventions.} Chain-of-thought prompting \citep{wei2022chain} improves reasoning by forcing intermediate steps. Constitutional AI \citep{bai2022constitutional} uses self-critique prompts to improve safety. \bodhi{} \citep{bodhi2026} uses a two-pass protocol: Pass~1 forces structured uncertainty analysis (clinical assessment, differentials, evidence quality, key uncertainties, questions to ask, scope boundaries, safe recommendations); Pass~2 generates a response conditioned on this analysis. The common limitation is deployment cost: the intervention must be applied at every inference call, consuming additional tokens and latency, with no guarantee that the underlying model has changed. We use the \bodhi{} CoT protocol not as the deployed intervention but as a demonstration source: the behavioral effect is distilled into model weights via fine-tuning, making the CoT protocol unnecessary at deployment.

\paragraph{LoRA and parameter-efficient fine-tuning.} LoRA \citep{hu2022lora} learns additive low-rank updates $\Delta W = BA$ with rank $r \ll \min(d,k)$. Variants include QLoRA \citep{dettmers2023qlora}, DoRA \citep{liu2024dora}, and rsLoRA \citep{kalajdzievski2023scaling}. LoRA is typically used for knowledge injection or task adaptation. We use it for \emph{behavioral modification}: the base model's knowledge is unchanged, but its manner of expressing that knowledge shifts toward calibrated uncertainty.

\paragraph{Self-improvement and behavioral distillation.} STaR \citep{zelikman2022star} bootstraps reasoning by filtering model-generated rationales on correctness; we apply the same filter-then-finetune logic to behavioral demonstrations rather than factual reasoning. Self-play fine-tuning \citep{chen2024selfplay} and iterative DPO \citep{rafailov2023direct, yuan2024selfrewarding} improve capabilities through repeated self-sampling and preference optimization; we differ in using a \emph{structured CoT protocol} to shift the generation distribution rather than self-sampling from the base policy or a reward model. Process reward models \citep{lightman2024lets} supervise individual reasoning steps; our rubric-graded filter similarly provides structured quality signal at the response level. RLAIF \citep{lee2024rlaif} shows that AI-generated preference labels can match human-level alignment signal---the asymmetric cross-family design we use (Qwen filter, Llama evaluator) extends this intuition to the grading pipeline, preventing same-model circularity. Standard distillation \citep{hinton2015distilling} and chain-of-thought distillation \citep{ho2023large, magister2023teaching} transfer from a larger teacher model to a smaller student; here teacher and student are the \emph{same} model under different inference conditions. The key design insight is using the CoT protocol as a behavioral teacher: it imposes a structured output distribution at inference time, and the adapter internalizes that distribution, making the CoT protocol unnecessary at deployment.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}
%======================================================================

\subsection{Overview}

Given a base model $M$, a structured CoT protocol $C$ that elicits target behaviors from $M$, and an evaluation rubric $R$ that can score the quality of $C(M)$'s outputs, the recipe proceeds in four steps:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Generate}: Run $C(M)$ on a set of prompts $\mathcal{P}$ to produce demonstrations $\{(x_i, y_i)\}$.
    \item \textbf{Grade}: Score each demonstration $y_i$ against $R$, obtaining quality scores $\{s_i\}$.
    \item \textbf{Filter}: Retain demonstrations where $s_i \geq \tau$, yielding training set $\mathcal{D} = \{(x_i, y_i) : s_i \geq \tau\}$.
    \item \textbf{Distill}: Fine-tune $M$ on $\mathcal{D}$ with LoRA, producing $M + \Delta C$ that approximates $C(M)$'s behavior without $C$ at inference.
\end{enumerate}

The key assumption is that $C(M)$ produces outputs of varying quality, and that $R$ can distinguish successful demonstrations from failures. The filter step ensures the adapter trains only on demonstrations where the CoT protocol elicited the target behavior. We instantiate this with $M$ = MedGemma-27B-text-it, $C$ = \bodhi{} CoT protocol, $R$ = HealthBench expert rubrics, $\tau = 0.4$, and $\Delta C$ = LoRA ($r{=}16$). Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} shows the full pipeline and the $2{\times}2$ evaluation design.

\subsection{Trace Generation}

We apply \bodhi{}'s two-pass CoT protocol to MedGemma-27B-text-it on 4,000 HealthBench-Full prompts, excluding all 1,000 Hard prompts by \texttt{prompt\_id} matching (verified by an automated preflight gate before every training job). Inference uses vLLM \citep{kwon2023efficient} on $8\times$ TPU v6e chips with greedy decoding. Each trace records input messages, Pass~1 analysis (internal uncertainty reasoning, not used for training), and Pass~2 response (the training target).

\subsection{Rubric Grading and Filtering}

A grader LLM (Qwen/Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct) evaluates each rubric criterion independently, producing a boolean \texttt{criteria\_met} judgment. We compute a normalized score:
\begin{equation}
    s = \frac{p_{\text{earned}} - p_{\text{neg}}}{p_{\text{pos}} - p_{\text{neg}}}
    \label{eq:score}
\end{equation}
where $p_{\text{pos}}, p_{\text{neg}}$ are sums of positive and negative rubric points and $p_{\text{earned}}$ is the sum for criteria met, bounding $s\in[0,1]$. Traces with $s \geq 0.4$ are retained, yielding ${\sim}$3,100 traces per seed (${\sim}$2,800 train / ${\sim}$310 val). $\tau{=}0.4$ was set \emph{a priori} as the 25th percentile of pilot scores on 500 traces drawn before any HealthBench-Hard evaluation, fixed across all 5 seeds, and not iterated on. The choice is failure-rejection rather than top-quality selection: the score distribution is broadly continuous over $[0.4, 0.7]$ with no regime boundary near the threshold (Appendix~\ref{app:robustness}, Table~\ref{tab:tau-calibration}), so $\tau \in [0.3, 0.5]$ produces training sets that differ in size but not composition. A full ablation across $\tau$ requires retraining and is reported as an open question.

\paragraph{Asymmetric grader design.} The filter (Qwen-14B) and evaluation grader (Llama-3.1-8B, \S\ref{sec:eval}) are different model families: any filter-grader bias affects only training data selection, not reported scores. The evaluator never participated in training-data selection.

\paragraph{Prompt-injection hardening.} The HealthBench grading template interpolates trace content between \texttt{<<conversation>>} delimiters; we escape template delimiters in interpolated content before grading.

\subsection{LoRA Fine-Tuning}

We fine-tune MedGemma-27B with LoRA ($r{=}16$, $\alpha{=}32$, dropout 0.05) on all 7 linear projections per layer (q, k, v, o, gate, up, down) using completion-only loss masking. Hyperparameters: 3 epochs, effective batch size 16, learning rate $10^{-4}$ (cosine, 5\% warmup), bfloat16. The 27B base is sharded across 8 TPU v6e chips via FSDPv2. We train 5 independent adapters (seeds 42, 7, 13, 99, 101) varying LoRA initialization, data order, and dropout masks; all results are mean $\pm$ std across these 5 seeds. Full configuration in Appendix~\ref{app:hparams}.

\subsection{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

\paragraph{Test set.} For each seed $k$, we draw 200 prompts without replacement from the 1,000 \hbhard{} examples. All 1,000 Hard prompts are excluded from training, so every possible 200-prompt draw is out-of-sample. The per-seed repeated subsampling captures both training noise (different LoRA init and data order) and eval-set-choice noise (different 200 prompts) in the reported variance.

\paragraph{Configurations.} We evaluate four conditions per seed in a $2 \times 2$ factorial design: Base, Base+\bodhi{} CoT, LoRA (no CoT), and LoRA+\bodhi{} CoT. This isolates the CoT effect on the base model (Base $\to$ Base+\bodhi{}), the weight-based effect of fine-tuning (Base $\to$ LoRA), the residual CoT value after fine-tuning (LoRA $\to$ LoRA+\bodhi{}), and potential interference between the two mechanisms.

\paragraph{Grading.} The evaluation grader is Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct. Evaluation was conducted on A100-80GB GPUs via Modal, with MedGemma-27B and the LoRA adapter merged on CPU before serving.

\paragraph{Epistemic virtue decomposition.} A separate evaluation pass grades each response on seven behavioral dimensions: (1) uncertainty acknowledgment (0--2), (2) active inquiry (binary question-asking rate), (3) context-seeking (0--2, substantiveness of questions), (4) red-flag identification (0--2), (5) scope bounding (0--2), (6) specificity (0--2), and (7) hedging quality (appropriate vs.\ blanket disclaimer). This decomposition distinguishes \emph{which} specific behaviors the adapter induces, beyond aggregate score changes.

The seven dimensions were chosen to cover the three epistemic functions a calibrated clinical response must serve. \emph{Self-assessment} (uncertainty acknowledgment, hedging quality, specificity) captures whether the model correctly represents what it does and does not know. \emph{Information-seeking} (active inquiry, context-seeking) captures the canonical clinical practice of gathering missing information before acting---operationalizing the principle that appropriate action under uncertainty requires first reducing it where possible \citep{simpkin2016tolerating}. \emph{Risk management} (red-flag identification, scope bounding) captures safety-netting: naming danger signs, specifying return thresholds, and bounding the scope of advice to what the model's evidence supports \citep{jones2019safety}. Together these cover epistemic behavior that is (a) observable in text without access to ground-truth clinical outcomes, (b) targeted by the \bodhi{} CoT protocol, and (c) neither redundant nor conflatable with aggregate quality score. We exclude dimensions like ``empathy'' or ``patient education depth'' that conflate \emph{what is communicated} with \emph{how accurately uncertainty is represented}.

\paragraph{Origin of the decomposition, and what each dimension looks like in text.} The seven
dimensions are not a renaming of \bodhi{}'s five components: \bodhi{} supplies the generation
protocol, while the decomposition is an evaluation instrument. The grouping above into
self-assessment, information-seeking and risk management was shaped by the practising physician
co-authors of this paper, who work in four health systems (US, Uganda, Colombia, UK) and who
identified these as the behaviors that most affect safety in clinical decision support. Because the
dimensions are graded from text, we state what each one looks like concretely:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt]
    \item \emph{Uncertainty acknowledgment}: ``without a chest X-ray I cannot confirm pneumonia.''
    \item \emph{Active inquiry}: ``when did the symptoms start?''
    \item \emph{Context-seeking}: ``I would need the patient's age and medication history.''
    \item \emph{Red-flag identification}: ``shortness of breath with chest pain warrants immediate
          evaluation.''
    \item \emph{Scope bounding}: ``I can give general information, but diagnosis requires an
          examination.''
    \item \emph{Hedging quality}: a specific qualified statement (``this could be either anxiety or a
          cardiac cause'') rather than a generic ``consult a doctor'' disclaimer.
    \item \emph{Specificity}: a concrete dose or timeframe (``325\,mg aspirin if no allergy'') rather
          than ``consider medication.''
\end{itemize}

The blanket-disclaimer rate is the key diagnostic for the ``hedging trap'': a model that has learned genuine calibration will have high appropriate-hedging rate and low blanket-disclaimer rate, while a model that has learned indiscriminate caution will have both rates high.

\paragraph{Integrity controls.} (1)~Contamination probe (Appendix~\ref{app:contamination}): 0/105 verbatim matches, mean prefix-overlap 0 tokens. (2)~Automated leakage gate ensures no evaluation prompt ID appears in training data. (3)~Grader parse-failure rate $<0.1\%$ across all reported runs (conservative not-met on failure). (4)~Physician validation partially returned (1 of 3 physicians; Appendix~\ref{app:human}): preliminary $\kappa{=}0.35$ physician vs.\ LLM, below the pre-registered target of $0.6$; aggregate-quality claims should be read as rubric-based proxy quality, not settled clinical validation.

\textbf{Clinical quality is preserved.} No pairwise comparison reaches significance at $\alpha=0.05$.

Table~\ref{tab:epistemic} shows the central pattern. \textbf{Internalization}: on every behavioral dimension, LoRA alone closely tracks the wrapper. Active inquiry 45.6\% vs.\ 47.9\%; bootstrap 95\% CI for the LoRA${-}$Wrapper difference is $[-0.069, +0.021]$, \emph{containing zero}---LoRA is statistically indistinguishable from the wrapper while requiring no inference-time CoT (5,000 resamples; Appendix~\ref{app:robustness}). \textbf{Interference}: stacking the wrapper on the adapter collapses red-flag rate from 78.3\% to 57.7\% (LoRA+\bodhi{}${-}$LoRA bootstrap CI $[-0.217, -0.101]$, robustly negative). Effect sizes for all seven dimensions are in Appendix~\ref{app:forest}. Statistics use prompt-level two-sample $t$-tests ($n\approx985$--$998$ per condition, pooled across 5 seeds); Bonferroni correction does not alter any significance conclusion at $d\geq0.20$.

\textbf{Active inquiry.} The largest effect. Base asks clarifying questions in 17.5\% of responses (0.45 questions per response). The LoRA adapter raises this to 45.6\% (1.16 questions/response), closely matching the CoT protocol's 47.9\% ($d = 0.63$, $p < 10^{-40}$). By Cohen's conventions this is a medium-to-large effect ($d=0.63$ exceeds the medium threshold of 0.5)---meaningful given that prompts within a condition vary substantially in difficulty and topic.

\textbf{Context-seeking.} The context-seeking score rises from 1.40 to 1.75 on the 0--2 scale ($d = 0.44$, $p < 10^{-20}$), capturing not just whether the model asks questions but how substantive they are.

\textbf{Scope bounding.} The adapter exceeds the CoT protocol: 97.6\% vs.\ 95.4\% (base: 92.7\%, $d = 0.22$, $p < 10^{-5}$).

\textbf{Appropriate hedging.} The blanket-disclaimer rate drops to 0.0\% (base: 0.5\%) and hedging quality rises from 1.86 to 1.91. The adapter produces targeted, contextually appropriate hedging rather than generic boilerplate.

\textbf{Red-flag identification.} The adapter raises red-flag identification from 1.52 to 1.67 on the 0--2 scale ($d = 0.20$, $p < 10^{-5}$). This safety-critical behavior---recognizing clinical warning signs that require escalation---transfers reliably to the adapted weights.

\textbf{Specificity is preserved.} The specificity rate is unchanged at 61.2\%, indicating the adapter does not sacrifice concrete clinical content (dosages, timeframes, referral specifics) in exchange for increased hedging. The model has not learned to replace substance with qualifications.

\textbf{Uncertainty acknowledgment.} Unexpectedly, the \bodhi{} CoT protocol modestly reduces uncertainty acknowledgment (Base: 1.94 $\to$ Base+\bodhi{}: 1.90), while the LoRA adapter leaves it unchanged (1.94). One possible explanation is that the CoT protocol's analysis pass reformulates uncertainty within the internal reasoning rather than foregrounding it in the patient-facing response. We flag this as an unresolved finding that warrants analysis of the Pass~1 analysis-field content.

\textbf{CoT--adapter interference.} When the CoT protocol is applied on top of the adapted model, active inquiry rises further to 80.0\%---but red-flag identification drops from 1.67 to 1.51 ($d = -0.25$, $p < 10^{-7}$), \emph{below} the unadapted base (1.52). The CoT protocol's analysis pass consumes context that the adapter's learned patterns would otherwise use for red-flag recognition. This is the clearest evidence of internalization: the two mechanisms compete for the same behavioral capacity.

\subsection{Response Characteristics and Inference Cost}

Base responses average ${\sim}3{,}220$ chars; LoRA $\sim 3{,}410$ ($+5.9$\%)---a 190-char increase cannot account for the 28~pp active-inquiry shift. The wrapper requires two inference passes (Pass~1 analysis $\sim$1,000 output tokens; Pass~2 response $\sim$1,018) totaling $\sim$2,020 output tokens per query; LoRA produces a single response at $\sim$851 tokens. Holding the behavioral target fixed (LoRA matches wrapper on every dimension, Table~\ref{tab:epistemic}), \textbf{LoRA delivers wrapper-equivalent behavior at ${\sim}58$\% lower inference output cost} and one inference call instead of two---the deployment payoff of internalization.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
%======================================================================

\paragraph{What the framework makes visible.} The adapter does not improve aggregate HealthBench-Hard ($d=0.03$, $p=0.57$), yet induces consistent prompt-level shifts in specific epistemic behaviors (Table~\ref{tab:epistemic}): active inquiry ($d=0.63$), context-seeking ($d=0.44$), scope bounding ($d=0.22$), red-flag ($d=0.20$), all $p<10^{-5}$. Stacking the CoT protocol on the adapter then degrades red-flag below the unadapted baseline ($d=-0.25$, $p<10^{-7}$)---legible only through the decomposition.

\paragraph{Calibration, not hedging.} The adapter produces targeted hedging rather than indiscriminate caution: blanket-disclaimer rate drops to 0.0\%, specificity is preserved (61.2\%), and appropriate-hedging rate rises from 90.6\% to 94.2\%. Difficulty stratification (Appendix~\ref{app:ushape}) shows failure rates rising monotonically with prompt difficulty in all conditions, consistent with the adapter modifying \emph{how} the model communicates rather than its underlying knowledge. Appendix~\ref{app:examples} illustrates these shifts at the response level.

\paragraph{CoT--adapter interference as a design constraint.} Red-flag identification drops from 1.67 (LoRA) to 1.51 (LoRA+\bodhi{}, $d=-0.25$, $p<10^{-7}$)---below the unadapted base (1.52). The pattern is asymmetric: CoT raises active inquiry in both conditions but suppresses red-flag only on the \emph{adapted} model. Figure~\ref{fig:interference} shows the mechanism: the adapter under CoT emits the protocol's Pass~1 analysis-format text rather than patient guidance; the two conditioning sources compete. Aggregate HealthBench is unchanged ($\Delta=0.008$, n.s.) while red-flag rate collapses 78.3\%${\to}$57.7\%---a safety-relevant regression invisible without the decomposition. If the adapter is deployed, CoT must be removed; harm-potential analysis (Appendix~\ref{app:harm}) is in progress.

\paragraph{What these behaviors mean clinically.} The dimensions are not abstract virtues; each
corresponds to a recognised clinical practice, which is what makes the shifts consequential.
\emph{Active inquiry} is history-taking: gathering the information that determines the answer before
committing to one, so a rise from 17.5\% to 45.6\% means the model more often behaves like a
clinician who asks before advising. \emph{Red-flag identification} is the recognition of
presentations that warrant escalation rather than reassurance, the behavior whose absence is most
directly linked to delayed care. \emph{Scope bounding} is declining to advise past what the
available evidence supports, which prevents a confident answer from substituting for an examination.
\emph{Hedging quality} separates a qualification that carries information (naming the specific
alternative under consideration) from a blanket disclaimer that carries none; the latter is what
makes a model feel cautious while remaining unhelpful.

\paragraph{Comparison to prior calibration approaches.} Prior work on calibration in language models
largely operates at inference time or on numerical confidence. \emph{Uncertainty prompting}
\citep{lin2022teaching, tian2023just} elicits verbalized confidence through the prompt, so the
behavior must be re-elicited at every call; we instead internalize it into weights, which removes the
per-call cost but also removes the ability to toggle it. \emph{STaR-style self-improvement}
\citep{zelikman2022star} shares our filter-then-finetune structure but filters on answer correctness,
whereas we filter behavioral demonstrations on rubric quality, which is why our filter can retain a
trace that is behaviorally exemplary but factually imperfect. \emph{Constitutional AI}
\citep{bai2022constitutional} also distills a prompted behavior into weights, but via self-critique
against written principles rather than a structured CoT protocol acting as a behavioral teacher. The
comparison we cannot yet make is empirical: we report no head-to-head against an inference-time
calibration baseline on the same benchmark and grader, and we regard that as the most important
missing experiment rather than a settled point in our favour.

\paragraph{Robustness.} The active-inquiry effect persists in every response-length quartile (shortest: LoRA 35.5\% vs.\ Base 25.3\%), ruling out verbosity. Internalization holds across the five most populated HealthBench themes within 0--7~pp on every theme. A parallel Qwen-14B grader run agrees with Llama on direction (LoRA${-}$Base ${\approx}0$, Wrapper${-}$Base slightly negative), supporting the aggregate-score framing across grader families (Appendix~\ref{app:robustness}).

\paragraph{Scope and limitations.} The headline results above are one instantiation: MedGemma-27B,
the \bodhi{} CoT protocol, and \hbhard{}. We report a replication across additional base models and
benchmarks in Appendix~\ref{app:generality}, which supports the transfer claim but also identifies a
precondition: the recipe requires a base model capable of following the CoT protocol, and produces
no effect where the protocol itself fails to elicit the behavior. What remains open is a single CoT
protocol (we vary the model and the benchmark, not the teacher); LLM-as-judge pending completion of
the physician validation (currently 1 of 3 raters, $\kappa=0.35$); no head-to-head comparison
against inference-time calibration baselines; greedy decoding only; and no $\tau$/rank/dataset-size
ablations. Our claim is therefore a recipe plus an evaluation decomposition for open-ended,
information-seeking clinical Q\&A, not a validated framework for task types where there is no
missing information to seek (for example closed-form multiple choice or summarization).
\url{https://anonymous.4open.science/r/bodhi-lora-CEB5}

\paragraph{Ethics and Broader Impact.} No patient data is used (public HealthBench only). The adapter is a research artifact, not for unsupervised clinical deployment. Positive impact: more calibrated clinical AI that asks before answering and recognizes danger signs. Primary risk: excessive hedging that delays emergency care---which is why we report red-flag identification per condition; the LoRA+\bodhi{} red-flag regression motivates the pre-registered clinician harm-potential review (Appendix~\ref{app:harm}). The released artifact requires gated MedGemma-27B access and inherits its license.

\section{Contamination Probe}
\label{app:contamination}

We test whether MedGemma-27B has memorized \hbhard{} prompts during pretraining. The protocol feeds the base model the first 15 tokens of each Hard prompt and asks for a 25-token continuation, then measures (a)~the exact-completion rate against the ground-truth continuation and (b)~the mean prefix-overlap in tokens. A second probe on PubMedQA questions provides a length-matched control distribution.

Of 200 Hard prompts sampled, 105 met the minimum-length requirement (prompt $\ge 40$ tokens). On those 105 prompts, the model produced \emph{zero} exact-match completions and a mean prefix-overlap of \emph{zero} tokens. The PubMedQA control probe produced no usable samples because PubMedQA questions are typically 10--20 tokens, below the 40-token threshold; we report the absolute zero on \hbhard{} alone and note the control baseline as a length-mismatch limitation. The headline finding---no detectable verbatim memorization on the prompts where the probe is well-defined---is consistent with no contamination. The probe data and per-prompt outputs are available with the rest of the artifacts.

\section{Human Expert Validation}
\label{app:human}

\section{Harm-Potential Analysis}
\label{app:harm}

Clinician review of high-stakes prompts is not yet completed and remains a pre-registered limitation. The intended protocol: identify HBHard prompts in four high-stakes categories (chest pain triage, anaphylaxis, suicide risk, drug-allergy contraindications), and have clinicians grade whether the response causes harmful delay-to-care relative to a base-model reference. Until those grades return, the central methodological risk---that the adapter trains the model to hedge in cases where confident, direct response would be clinically appropriate---cannot be ruled out. We flag the \emph{LoRA+\bodhi{}} red-flag-identification regression (57.7\% vs.\ 78.3\% for LoRA alone) as an early warning sign that motivates this analysis.

On those paired samples, Pearson $r = 0.42$ pooled across conditions ($r = 0.35, 0.37, 0.51$ for Base, Wrapper, LoRA respectively); Spearman $\rho = 0.34$. The two graders disagree on absolute scale (Qwen mean ${\approx} 0.15$, Llama mean ${\approx} 0.47$) but agree on direction: LoRA${-}$Base is ${\approx} 0$ in both graders ($-0.008$ Qwen, $-0.004$ Llama), and Wrapper${-}$Base is slightly negative in both ($-0.036$ Qwen, $-0.012$ Llama). This supports the paper's framing on aggregate scores (both graders agree the adapter does not change them) but does not speak to the behavioral decomposition, which is graded only by Llama; the closed loop on 0--2 dimensions remains pending physician validation.

\paragraph{Behavioral dimensions are statistically distinct.} Per-prompt Pearson correlations between the LoRA${-}$Base shift in active inquiry and the corresponding shifts in other dimensions ($n{=}983$ prompts with all 5 dimensions in both conditions): $r{=}{+}0.23$ with context-seeking (consistent with both involving question-asking), $r{=}{-}0.08$ with red-flag identification, $r{=}{+}0.02$ with scope bounding, $r{=}{-}0.02$ with specificity. The dimensions are not redundant---the adapter can shift active inquiry on a prompt without simultaneously shifting red-flag identification, scope, or specificity, supporting their treatment as separate behavioral targets.

\section{Generality: Additional Base Models and Benchmarks}
\label{app:generality}

The main results instantiate the recipe on one model and one benchmark. To test whether the
behavioral transfer depends on that choice, we re-ran the pipeline unchanged, holding every
hyperparameter fixed (LoRA $r{=}16$, $\alpha{=}32$, effective batch 16, 3 epochs, $\tau{=}0.4$,
Qwen-14B filter, Llama-3.1-8B evaluator), on two additional base models and two additional
benchmarks, with 5 seeds per cell and ${\sim}200$ prompts per seed per condition.

The added base models are \textbf{Mistral-Small-24B-Instruct} (general-purpose, Mistral family) and
\textbf{Med42-8B} (clinically tuned, Llama-3 family), spanning both a different family from the
original MedGemma-27B (Gemma) and the clinical/general distinction. The added benchmarks are
\textbf{ChatDoctor} (HealthCareMagic; unedited questions real patients asked physicians online) and
\textbf{MedQuAD} (NIH consumer-health QA), which differ from \hbhard{} in source and in how much
information the question withholds. Benchmarks without native expert rubrics are filtered against
rubrics synthesized from their ground truth (reference answer or known-correct option), graded by
the same Qwen-14B filter, preserving the asymmetric cross-family design.

\paragraph{The behavioral transfer is not specific to MedGemma or HealthBench.} Active inquiry and
context-seeking rise in every capable configuration, and in four of the five the adapter matches or
exceeds its own teacher while requiring no CoT at inference. The magnitude tracks how much the
benchmark withholds: the largest shift is on ChatDoctor with Med42-8B (2.9\% to 86.4\%), where real
patient messages routinely omit age, duration and medications, and the smallest is on the
better-specified MedQuAD questions (6.5\% to 26.5\%).

\paragraph{A precondition, not a universal recipe.} BioMistral-7B shows no effect. The diagnostic is
that the \emph{teacher} also fails on this base: the CoT wrapper reaches only 14.5\% active inquiry,
versus 54--83\% on the other models. Where the protocol cannot elicit the behavior, there is nothing
to distill. We therefore state a scope condition: the recipe requires a base model capable of
following the structured CoT protocol.

\paragraph{Distillation filters the teacher rather than copying it.} On MedQuAD the wrapper
\emph{degrades} two dimensions relative to base (scope bounding $1.85\to1.61$, hedging quality
$1.45\to1.40$) while the distilled adapter \emph{improves} both ($1.89$ and $1.78$). The
quality-filtering step removes the teacher's failures before they reach the weights, which is
evidence against a pure imitation account of what the adapter learns.

\paragraph{Aggregate quality.} On \hbhard{} the adapter's aggregate rubric score is essentially
unchanged (Mistral-Small-24B $0.425\to0.411$; Med42-8B $0.405\to0.392$), consistent with the
non-inferiority result in Table~\ref{tab:main}. On the new benchmarks there are modest decreases,
largest on MedQuAD ($0.672\to0.586$). We read this as partly a property of the rubric rather than of
the model: MedQuAD's synthesized rubric rewards agreement with a reference answer, so a response
that asks a clarifying question instead of answering scores lower by construction. Consistent with
that reading, the wrapper, which asks most, drops furthest of all ($0.672\to0.542$). This is the
measurement problem the paper is about, and it is why we report the behavioral decomposition
alongside the aggregate rather than in place of it.

\section{Qualitative Examples (4-condition comparison)}
\label{app:examples}

Examples are drawn from seed~42 evaluation outputs on \hbhard{}, with scores from the Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct epistemic grader. All prompts and responses are verbatim and reproducible from \texttt{data/raw/healthbench\_hard.jsonl} (filter by \texttt{prompt\_id}) and \texttt{results\_modal/seed\_42/epistemic\_scores.json}. \texttt{[\ldots]} marks omitted text; nothing is paraphrased. Each example shows all four evaluation conditions: Base (no wrapper), Wrapper (Base+\bodhi{} CoT), LoRA (no wrapper), LoRA+Wrapper.

\paragraph{Example A: Scope bounding under user pressure (mask question, \texttt{prompt\_id 7eba4984}).}

After two prior turns where the assistant gave nuanced answers, the user demands a binary verdict.

\convlabel{User:}I just want a yes or no, that's all
\end{tcolorbox}

\convlabel{Assistant:}Okay, based on the general public health recommendations for reducing the spread of respiratory viruses like the flu, the answer is \textbf{yes}. Wearing a mask is a helpful measure, especially in crowded indoor settings or when respiratory illnesses are circulating widely.

\begin{tcolorbox}[convbox, fontupper=\footnotesize,

[\ldots] the most direct answer to ``Should I wear a mask?'' is \textbf{yes}, it can be a helpful measure, especially during flu season. However, [\ldots] this isn't a one-size-fits-all recommendation. The \emph{best} decision for \emph{you} depends on your personal circumstances [\ldots] \textbf{If you are feeling sick or have flu symptoms (like fever, cough, sore throat, body aches), the answer is a strong YES.} [\ldots] \textbf{If you have underlying health conditions that put you at higher risk for flu complications [\ldots], wearing a mask in public is generally recommended.} [\ldots] \textbf{To help me give you more tailored advice, could you tell me:} (1)~Are you currently feeling sick? High (in the general effectiveness of masks for reducing droplet spread). [\ldots] \textbf{6. RED FLAGS: None} [\ldots] \textbf{7. SAFE RECOMMENDATIONS:} [\ldots] [\emph{continues into a SOAP-note draft; original yes/no remains unanswered for the patient}]

\textbf{Reading the comparison.} Base alone gives an unconditional ``yes'' that ignores the meaningful asymmetry in mask utility (sick vs.\ healthy, high-risk vs.\ low-risk). Wrapper produces a SOAP-note structure that refuses the binary entirely. LoRA alone matches the wrapper's behavioral target while presenting it as a patient-facing response: gives the binary, then immediately scope-bounds to risk strata, asks the three personalizing questions a clinician would ask, and lists emergency warning signs. LoRA+Wrapper reverts to internal-analysis format and never produces an actionable response.

\begin{tcolorbox}[convbox, fontupper=\footnotesize,

\textbf{Reading the comparison.} Base fabricates a specific etiology (``unknown genetic variant or a rare hematologic condition'') unsupported by the data---a textbook confabulation under user pressure (red\_flag=0, scope=0, hedging inappropriate). Wrapper fabricates with structure: it commits to ``benign, novel genetic variant'' and produces a full SOAP plan, but then explicitly flags the diagnosis as ``highly speculative'' and reliant on assumed normal labs. LoRA alone gives the most epistemically honest answer: bounds the response to what the lone data point can support (a metabolic abnormality, no specific diagnosis) and explicitly enumerates what cannot be inferred. The contrast between Base and LoRA is the signature distinction this paper targets: confident-sounding confabulation vs.\ principled abstention from claims the data don't license.
