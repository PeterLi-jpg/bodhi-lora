\begin{abstract}
Careful clinicians ask for missing facts before they advise, name the warning signs that need
urgent care, and say what an answer cannot cover. Clinical language models often answer at once
instead. We study whether a model can learn these epistemic behaviors into its weights, for
patient-facing, open-ended clinical questions. We contribute two things. The first is a
decomposition of epistemic behavior into seven dimensions that an LLM grades from the response
text: uncertainty acknowledgment, active inquiry, context-seeking, red-flag identification, scope
bounding, hedging quality and specificity. The second is a recipe: the \bodhi{} chain-of-thought
(CoT) protocol generates demonstrations, a rubric filter from one model family keeps the good ones,
a LoRA adapter learns them, and an evaluator from a second family grades the result. On
MedGemma-27B and \hbhard{} (\statNSeeds{} seeds, \statUniquePrompts{} unique prompts), the adapter
without CoT raises active inquiry from \statActiveInquiryBase\% to \statActiveInquiryLoRA\%
(difference \statActiveInquiryLoRAvsBaseDiff{} points, 95\% CI \statActiveInquiryLoRAvsBaseCI) and
context-seeking from \statContextSeekingBase{} to \statContextSeekingLoRA{} on a 0--2 scale. In this
cell we detect no change in the aggregate rubric score (\statHealthBenchLoRAvsBaseDiff, 95\% CI
\statHealthBenchLoRAvsBaseCI). In the \statCellsAddedCompared{} added cells whose base model is
outside the evaluator's family, active inquiry rises in \statCellsAddedAIRose{}, and the aggregate
score falls in all \statCellsAddedWithAggregate{}, by \statCellsAddedDropMin{} to
\statCellsAddedDropMax{} (values transcribed from posted results; raw outputs pending sync). The
behavior gain therefore comes with an aggregate cost. Adding the CoT protocol to the adapted model
lowers red-flag identification; a post hoc analysis links the drop to the protocol's analysis
format leaking into \statLeakagePctLoRACoT\% of the answers. All behavior scores are LLM-graded: the one
physician who returned grades agrees with the grader at $\kappa = \statKappaAll$, below our
pre-registered target of 0.6.
\end{abstract}

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} clinical language models; epistemic behaviors; behavioral evaluation; LoRA fine-tuning; chain-of-thought distillation; epistemic uncertainty; active inquiry; medical AI safety; HealthBench; self-distillation.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
%======================================================================

A clinical language model that scores well on multiple-choice benchmarks may still answer a
patient's question without asking for the facts it needs. Careful clinicians ask before they
advise, hedge where the evidence is thin, name danger signs, and say when the patient needs an
examination. Medical education treats tolerance of uncertainty as part of expert judgment
\citep{simpkin2016tolerating}, and safety-netting (telling a patient what to watch for and when to
return) as a core clinical skill \citep{jones2019safety}. Language models trained with RLHF tend
toward confident outputs \citep{kadavath2022language, lin2022teaching}, in part because human
raters prefer decisive answers \citep{xiong2024can}, and current clinical models inherit this
tendency \citep{ouyang2022training, wornow2023shaky}. Aggregate clinical scores also mix
\emph{what} a model knows with \emph{how} it communicates, so a response that omits a warning sign
can score as well as one that names it.

Our starting point is the \bodhi{} protocol (\textbf{B}alanced, \textbf{O}pen-minded,
\textbf{D}iagnostic, \textbf{H}umble, \textbf{I}nquisitive) \citep{bodhi2026}, a two-pass
chain-of-thought (CoT) protocol. Pass~1 writes an internal analysis (clinical assessment,
differentials, evidence quality, key uncertainties, questions to ask, scope boundaries, safe
recommendations). Pass~2 writes the answer from that analysis. The protocol elicits the target
behaviors at inference time, but it needs two generation passes, and in our evaluation the two
passes overran the context window on \statCompletionDropPctBaseCoT\% of prompts. We use the
protocol as a source of demonstrations instead, and ask whether a LoRA adapter can learn the
behaviors so that the protocol is not needed at deployment.

Our setting is patient-facing, open-ended clinical question answering. In \hbhard{},
\statPostedClinicianPrompts{} of \statHardPrompts{} prompts say that the speaker is a clinician
(explicit self-identification only, so this is a lower bound). We do not study clinician-posed
settings such as MIMIC or eICU, and we do not measure calibration in the statistical sense: we
measure behaviors that are visible in the answer.

Our contributions:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item A decomposition of epistemic behavior into seven dimensions, graded from the response
    text, with a grading design that separates model families: a Qwen2.5-14B filter selects the
    training data and a Llama-3.1-8B evaluator grades the results (\S\ref{sec:method}). The
    separation removes the most direct route to circularity. It does not rule out correlated
    grading preferences, and the grades are not clinically validated (\S\ref{sec:eval}).
    \item A recipe (generate, grade, filter, distill) and its evaluation on MedGemma-27B over
    \statNSeeds{} seeds, with the seed as the unit of every test. Without CoT at inference, the
    adapter raises active inquiry, context-seeking, red-flag identification and scope bounding
    (Holm-adjusted $p \leq \statScopeBoundingLoRAvsBasePHolm$). In added cells with other base
    models and benchmarks, the rise in active inquiry repeats in \statCellsAddedAIRose{} of
    \statCellsAddedCompared{} cells outside the evaluator's family, and the aggregate rubric score
    falls in all \statCellsAddedWithAggregate{} of them (\S\ref{sec:cells}). We report this
    trade-off next to the gain.
    \item A diagnosis of a failure mode: adding the CoT protocol to the adapted model lowers
    red-flag identification. A post hoc analysis ties the drop to the protocol's analysis format
    leaking into the answer (\S\ref{sec:interference}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
%======================================================================

\paragraph{Calibration and confidence in language models.} \citet{kadavath2022language} showed
that LLMs hold internal uncertainty signals that probing can read, while RLHF-tuned outputs often
hide them. Preference labels favor decisive answers \citep{xiong2024can}, so instruction tuning
with human feedback \citep{ouyang2022training} can penalize appropriate hedging.
\citet{lin2022teaching} fine-tuned models to state their confidence in words, and
\citet{tian2023just} got similar effects from prompting alone. These works target numerical
confidence. We target behaviors in the answer (hedging, asking, declining), and we do not measure
calibration in the statistical sense. A model can have a low expected calibration error and still
omit the safety-netting questions a practitioner would ask.

\paragraph{Clinical epistemic behaviors.} \citet{simpkin2016tolerating} argue that clinicians must
learn to act well under uncertainty that they cannot remove, instead of seeking false certainty.
Safety-netting, telling patients what to watch for and when to return \citep{jones2019safety}, is
a core clinical behavior; our red-flag and scope-bounding dimensions put it into a grading rubric.
\citet{wornow2023shaky} report that clinical LLMs trained on health records inherit the confident
style of clinical notes rather than the way a clinician explains uncertainty to a patient.

\paragraph{Medical LLM evaluation.} Standard medical benchmarks (MedQA, MMLU-Medical) test factual
accuracy with multiple-choice questions. HealthBench \citep{healthbench2025} grades open-ended
answers against expert rubrics with positive criteria (correct content, appropriate referral) and
negative criteria (dangerous advice, false reassurance, failure to escalate). It penalizes both
overconfidence and unhelpful hedging. We use its grading protocol, escape template delimiters in
graded text, and grade with a model family other than the filter's.

\paragraph{Inference-time behavioral interventions.} Chain-of-thought prompting
\citep{wei2022chain} adds intermediate steps; Constitutional AI \citep{bai2022constitutional}
uses self-critique against written principles. Interventions at inference time must run at every
call, which costs tokens and latency, and they leave the model unchanged. We use the \bodhi{} CoT
protocol as a teacher instead: its outputs become training data, so the deployed adapter does not
need the protocol.

\paragraph{Parameter-efficient fine-tuning and distillation.} LoRA \citep{hu2022lora} learns
low-rank updates $\Delta W = BA$; variants include QLoRA \citep{dettmers2023qlora}, DoRA
\citep{liu2024dora} and rsLoRA \citep{kalajdzievski2023scaling}. We use LoRA to change behavior,
not to add knowledge. STaR \citep{zelikman2022star} filters model-generated rationales on
correctness and fine-tunes on the rest; we apply the same filter-then-finetune logic to behavioral
demonstrations, filtered on rubric quality. Self-play and iterative preference optimization
\citep{chen2024selfplay, rafailov2023direct, yuan2024selfrewarding} sample from the model's own
policy; we shift the generation distribution with a structured protocol. Process reward models
\citep{lightman2024lets} grade single reasoning steps; our filter grades whole responses. RLAIF
\citep{lee2024rlaif} shows that AI-generated labels can stand in for human ones; we grade the
filter and the evaluation with different model families to reduce same-model circularity. In
standard and chain-of-thought distillation \citep{hinton2015distilling, ho2023large,
magister2023teaching}, a larger teacher trains a smaller student. Here the teacher and the student
are the same model under different inference conditions.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}
%======================================================================

\subsection{Overview}

Given a base model $M$, a structured CoT protocol $C$ that elicits target behaviors from $M$, and
a rubric $R$ that scores the outputs of $C(M)$, the recipe has four steps:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt]
    \item \textbf{Generate}: run $C(M)$ on a prompt set $\mathcal{P}$ to produce demonstrations $\{(x_i, y_i)\}$.
    \item \textbf{Grade}: score each demonstration $y_i$ against $R$, giving scores $\{s_i\}$.
    \item \textbf{Filter}: keep demonstrations with $s_i \geq \tau$, giving $\mathcal{D} = \{(x_i, y_i) : s_i \geq \tau\}$.
    \item \textbf{Distill}: fine-tune $M$ on $\mathcal{D}$ with LoRA, giving $M + \Delta C$, which should
    show the behavior of $C(M)$ without $C$ at inference.
\end{enumerate}

The recipe assumes that $C(M)$ produces outputs of varying quality and that $R$ can tell good
demonstrations from failures. We use $M$ = MedGemma-27B-text-it, $C$ = the \bodhi{} CoT protocol,
$R$ = the HealthBench expert rubrics, $\tau = 0.4$ and $\Delta C$ = LoRA ($r{=}8$).
Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} shows the pipeline and the $2{\times}2$ evaluation design.

\subsection{Trace Generation, Grading and Filtering}

We run the two-pass \bodhi{} protocol on MedGemma-27B-text-it over the \statFilterPool{}
HealthBench prompts that are not in the Hard set, with greedy decoding in vLLM
\citep{kwon2023efficient} on TPU v6e. A preflight gate checks that no Hard prompt id enters
training. In the one training split kept in the repository (seed 7, \statFilterTrainNSeedSeven{}
traces), the overlap with the Hard set is \statTrainHardOverlapSeedSeven{} prompt ids. Each trace keeps the
Pass~1 analysis (not used for training) and the Pass~2 answer (the training target).

A grader LLM (Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct) judges each rubric criterion as met or not met, and we compute
\begin{equation}
    s = \frac{p_{\text{earned}} - p_{\text{neg}}}{p_{\text{pos}} - p_{\text{neg}}}
    \label{eq:score}
\end{equation}
where $p_{\text{pos}}$ and $p_{\text{neg}}$ are the sums of positive and negative rubric points and
$p_{\text{earned}}$ is the sum over met criteria, so $s\in[0,1]$. We keep traces with $s \geq 0.4$.
We fixed $\tau = 0.4$ before the \hbhard{} evaluation and used it for all five seeds. It removes
failures rather than selecting only the best traces (Table~\ref{tab:tau-calibration}). We did not
run an ablation over $\tau$.

\paragraph{Separate grader families.} The filter (Qwen2.5-14B) and the evaluator (Llama-3.1-8B,
\S\ref{sec:eval}) come from different model families, and the evaluator takes no part in selecting
training data. This removes the most direct route to circularity. It does not rule out grading
preferences that the two families share, and it gives no clinical validity. Two of the added cells
(\S\ref{sec:cells}) use Med42-8B, a Llama-3 model, and so break this separation.

\subsection{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

\paragraph{Test set and seeds.} Each seed draws 200 of the \statHardPrompts{} \hbhard{} prompts,
and its four conditions share that draw. No Hard prompt is used in training. The draws overlap:
the \statPromptEvaluations{} draws per condition cover \statUniquePrompts{} unique prompts, and
\statRepeatedPrompts{} prompts appear in more than one seed. A seed sets the LoRA initialization,
the data order, the dropout masks, the train/validation split and the evaluation draw. It does not
vary the teacher traces or their filter scores: we generated the traces once and graded them
greedily.

\paragraph{Conditions.} The $2 \times 2$ design has four conditions per seed: Base, Base+\bodhi{}
(the base model with the CoT protocol, which is also the teacher), LoRA (no CoT) and LoRA+\bodhi{}.
It separates the effect of the protocol on the base model, the effect of fine-tuning, and their
interaction.

\paragraph{Grading.} Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct grades every response twice: once per HealthBench
rubric criterion, which gives the aggregate score of Eq.~\ref{eq:score}, and once on the seven
epistemic dimensions. Generation ran in vLLM on A100-80GB GPUs (Modal) with a 4,096-token context
and greedy decoding, with the adapter merged into the base weights.

\paragraph{Seven epistemic dimensions.} The epistemic pass grades (1) uncertainty acknowledgment
(0--2), (2) active inquiry (whether the answer asks at least one direct clarifying question), (3)
context-seeking (0--2; a 2 needs a direct question in a case that needs more context), (4)
red-flag identification (0--2), (5) scope bounding
(0--2), (6) specificity (0--2) and (7) hedging quality (0--2, with flags for appropriate hedging
and for a blanket disclaimer). The dimensions cover three functions. \emph{Self-assessment}
(uncertainty acknowledgment, hedging quality, specificity) asks whether the answer represents what
the model does and does not know. \emph{Information-seeking} (active inquiry, context-seeking)
asks whether it gathers missing facts before it advises \citep{simpkin2016tolerating}. \emph{Risk
management} (red-flag identification, scope bounding) covers safety-netting
\citep{jones2019safety}. Every dimension is visible in text without clinical outcomes. The grader's anchors address the
person who asks (for example ``Are you experiencing chest pain right now?''), so no dimension
assumes a clinician reader. We chose the seven dimensions by informal consensus in the author
group, which includes three practising physicians. We used no formal method (no Delphi round, no
content-validity index). They are not a renaming of the five \bodhi{} components: \bodhi{} is the
generation protocol, and the decomposition is an evaluation instrument. We left out candidates such
as empathy and depth of patient education, which mix what is said with how uncertainty is
represented. Appendix~\ref{app:dimensions} gives a text example for each dimension.

\paragraph{Statistical analysis.} The seed is the unit of inference. For each contrast between two
conditions, we take the five per-seed differences of condition means and report their mean with a
95\% $t$ interval (4 df) and a paired $t$-test. Within each contrast we correct the seven
dimensions with Holm's method. Pooled Cohen's $d$ over responses is descriptive; its 95\% interval
comes from a bootstrap that resamples prompt ids, because the draws overlap. We set no equivalence
margin, so a non-significant difference is not evidence that two conditions are equal.
Appendix~\ref{app:stats} gives every contrast.

\paragraph{Grading failures.} The epistemic grader failed to parse \statEpistemicParseFailPctBase\%
to \statEpistemicParseFailPctMax\% of the graded responses, depending on the condition
(Table~\ref{tab:counts}); those responses leave the epistemic means. A worst-case analysis gives
every failed or missing grade the worst value of its scale (Table~\ref{tab:worst-case}). The
HealthBench grader failed on at most \statHealthBenchParseFailPctMaxRun\% of the criteria in any
run, and a failed criterion counts as not met.

\paragraph{Physician check.} The pre-registered check needed at least two physicians and
$\kappa \geq 0.6$ against their consensus. Of the three physicians, \statKappaRaters{}
returned grades. On \statKappaAllN{} criterion rows from \statKappaPrompts{} prompts, Cohen's $\kappa$
between that physician and the Llama grader is \statKappaAll{} (95\% CI \statKappaAllCI, bootstrap
over prompts), below the target. We record this amendment in Appendix~\ref{app:human}. Every
epistemic result in this paper is therefore LLM-graded and not clinically validated.

differences stay positive with $p \leq \statScopeBoundingLoRAvsBaseWorstCaseP$
(Table~\ref{tab:worst-case}).

\paragraph{Against the teacher.} On active inquiry, LoRA alone differs from Base+\bodhi{} by
\statActiveInquiryLoRAvsBaseCoTDiff{} points (95\% CI \statActiveInquiryLoRAvsBaseCoTCI). The
lower end of the interval is \statActiveInquiryLoRAvsBaseCoTCILow{} points, so the interval does not
show that the two are equal. The adapter needs one generation pass and answered every prompt; the
teacher needs two passes and gave no answer on \statCompletionDropPctBaseCoT\% of prompts.

\paragraph{Aggregate score.} In this cell the aggregate score changes by
\statHealthBenchLoRAvsBaseDiff{} under the adapter (95\% CI \statHealthBenchLoRAvsBaseCI; $p =
\statHealthBenchLoRAvsBaseP$; Table~\ref{tab:main}). We detect no change, but the lower end of the
interval, \statHealthBenchLoRAvsBaseCILow, leaves room for a small drop. Over pooled completions, the rubric
accuracy axis moves from \statAxisAccuracyBase{} to \statAxisAccuracyLoRA{} and completeness from
\statAxisCompletenessBase{} to \statAxisCompletenessLoRA{}, while communication quality falls from
\statAxisCommunicationQualityBase{} to \statAxisCommunicationQualityLoRA{} and instruction following
rises from \statAxisInstructionFollowingBase{} to \statAxisInstructionFollowingLoRA{}
(Table~\ref{tab:axis}).

\paragraph{Disclaimers and length.} Base gave \statBlanketDisclaimerCountBase{} blanket
disclaimers in \statNScoredBase{} graded responses, and LoRA gave \statBlanketDisclaimerCountLoRA{}
in \statNScoredLoRA{} (two-sided Fisher exact $p = \statBlanketDisclaimerFisherPLoRA$;
Table~\ref{tab:disclaimers}). These are too few events to show a difference. LoRA answers differ in length
from Base by \statResponseCharsLoRAvsBaseDiff{} characters on average (95\% CI
\statResponseCharsLoRAvsBaseCI). The rise in active inquiry appears in every quartile of response
length (Table~\ref{tab:length}), so length does not explain it within those quartiles. We ran no
length-controlled test.

\subsection{The CoT Protocol on the Adapted Model}
\label{sec:interference}

Adding the CoT protocol to the adapter raises active inquiry further, to
\statActiveInquiryLoRACoT\%, but lowers red-flag identification from \statRedFlagLoRA{} to
\statRedFlagLoRACoT{} (difference \statRedFlagLoRACoTvsLoRADiff, 95\% CI
\statRedFlagLoRACoTvsLoRACI; $p = \statRedFlagLoRACoTvsLoRAP$, Holm-adjusted
\statRedFlagLoRACoTvsLoRAPHolm). The red-flag rate changes by \statRedFlagRateLoRACoTvsLoRADiff{}
points (\statRedFlagRateLoRACoTvsLoRACI; $p = \statRedFlagRateLoRACoTvsLoRAP$, outside the Holm
family). Against Base, LoRA+\bodhi{} is level on the 0--2 score (\statRedFlagLoRACoTvsBaseDiff,
\statRedFlagLoRACoTvsBaseCI) and lower on the rate (\statRedFlagRateLoRACoTvsBaseDiff{} points,
\statRedFlagRateLoRACoTvsBaseCI). Its aggregate score differs from LoRA by
\statHealthBenchLoRACoTvsLoRADiff{} (\statHealthBenchLoRACoTvsLoRACI).

A post hoc analysis points to format leakage. In \statLeakagePctLoRACoT\% of LoRA+\bodhi{}
responses, the answer repeats the headers of the protocol's Pass~1 analysis, against
\statLeakagePctBase\% to \statLeakagePctBaseCoT\% in the other conditions
(Figure~\ref{fig:interference} shows one case). Leaked responses score \statRedFlagLeaked{} on
red-flag identification ($n = \statLeakageNLoRACoT$); the others score \statRedFlagClean{} ($n =
\statLeakageNCleanLoRACoT$), close to LoRA alone. We defined the leakage split after we saw the
results, by a property of the format, so leakage may also follow prompt difficulty. We do not claim
that the adapter and the protocol compete for capacity. In practice, the adapter should run
without the protocol.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
%======================================================================

\paragraph{The trade-off.} The adapter changes how the model answers: it asks more, names warning
signs more often and bounds its scope. In the submitted cell we detect no change in aggregate
rubric score; in the \statCellsAddedWithAggregate{} added cells outside the evaluator's family,
the score falls by \statCellsAddedDropMin{} to \statCellsAddedDropMax. Whether the gain is worth the cost depends on the deployment risk. Where a
missed warning sign or advice built on missing facts is costly, as in patient-facing triage, more
inquiry can be worth a small loss in rubric score. Where users ask well-specified questions and
expect a complete answer, as in MedQuAD, the cost is larger and the gain smaller. A deployment
should measure both on its own prompts before it chooses.

\paragraph{What these behaviors mean clinically.} Each dimension maps to a clinical practice.
\emph{Active inquiry} is history-taking: getting the facts that decide the answer before giving
it. \emph{Red-flag identification} is recognizing presentations that need escalation, not
reassurance; its absence is the one most directly linked to delayed care. \emph{Scope bounding} is
declining to advise past the available evidence, so that a confident answer does not replace an
examination. \emph{Hedging quality} separates a qualification that carries information (it names
the alternative under consideration) from a blanket disclaimer that carries none. Active inquiry
and context-seeking help only where facts are missing and the user can give them; scope bounding
and red-flag identification apply to any clinical question. Outside the evaluator's family, the
rise in active inquiry is largest on ChatDoctor, unedited patient messages
(\statCellChatDoctorMistralBaseActiveInquiry\% to \statCellChatDoctorMistralLoRAActiveInquiry\%),
and smallest among the cells where it rises on MedQuAD, short reference questions
(\statCellMedQuADMistralBaseActiveInquiry\% to \statCellMedQuADMistralLoRAActiveInquiry\%).

\paragraph{Comparison to prior approaches.} Uncertainty prompting \citep{lin2022teaching,
tian2023just} works through the prompt, so it must run at every call; the recipe changes the
weights, which removes that cost and also removes the option to turn the behavior off.
STaR-style self-improvement \citep{zelikman2022star} shares the filter-then-finetune structure but
filters on answer correctness; we filter on rubric quality. Constitutional AI
\citep{bai2022constitutional} also distills a prompted behavior into weights, through self-critique
against written principles rather than a structured protocol. We report no empirical comparison
with an inference-time baseline. Code for two such baselines (a clarifying-question prompt and a
verbalized-confidence prompt) is in the repository but has not been run. We regard this as the most
important missing experiment.

\paragraph{Limitations.} We tested one CoT protocol (\bodhi{}) and one fine-tuning method (LoRA).
We make no claim about other protocols, other methods, or tasks with no missing information to
seek, such as closed-form multiple choice, extraction or summarization. Every behavior score is
LLM-graded, and the one returned physician agrees with the grader below the pre-registered target.
The added-cell values are transcribed from posted replies until the raw outputs are synced, and
those cells used another recipe and other evaluation settings. Red-flag identification measures
sensitivity only; over-flagging needs prompts with no true red flag. Decoding was greedy, and we ran
no ablation over $\tau$, rank or data size. The seeds vary training and the evaluation draw but not
the teacher traces. The LoRA configuration of the submitted adapters comes from the training code
and awaits a direct check against the saved adapter files.

\paragraph{Ethics and broader impact.} We use public HealthBench data and no patient data. The
adapter is a research artifact, not a tool for unsupervised clinical use. The main risk is hedging
or asking that delays urgent care, which is why we report red-flag identification for each
condition; the drop under LoRA+\bodhi{} motivates the clinician harm review in
Appendix~\ref{app:harm}, which is not yet done. The adapter needs gated access to MedGemma-27B and
inherits its license.

\paragraph{Code availability.} Code, adapters and evaluation scripts are at
\url{https://anonymous.4open.science/r/bodhi-lora-CEB5}.

\section{Contamination Probe}
\label{app:contamination}

We tested whether MedGemma-27B had memorized \hbhard{} prompts in pretraining. The probe
(\texttt{scripts/contamination\_probe.py}) gives the base model the first 15 tokens of a Hard prompt
and asks for a 25-token continuation, then measures the exact-completion rate and the mean prefix
overlap in tokens. Of 200 sampled Hard prompts, 105 met the minimum length of 40 tokens. On those
105, the model produced no exact completion and a mean prefix overlap of zero tokens, which shows
no verbatim memorization where the probe is defined. A PubMedQA control gave no usable samples,
because its questions are shorter than 40 tokens. The probe's output file was not kept in the
repository, so these counts cannot be re-derived from it; re-running the probe needs the base
model on a GPU.

\section{Harm-Potential Analysis}
\label{app:harm}

Clinician review of high-stakes prompts is not done. The planned protocol selects \hbhard{} prompts
in four high-stakes categories (chest-pain triage, anaphylaxis, suicide risk, drug-allergy
contraindications) and asks clinicians whether a response delays care harmfully, compared with
the base model. Until that review is done, we cannot rule out that the adapter hedges or asks where
a direct answer is the right one. The drop in red-flag rate from LoRA (\statRedFlagRateLoRA\%) to
LoRA+\bodhi{} (\statRedFlagRateLoRACoT\%) is an early warning that motivates the review.

\section{Example of Each Dimension}
\label{app:dimensions}

Because the dimensions are graded from text, we state what each one looks like:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt]
    \item \emph{Uncertainty acknowledgment}: ``without a chest X-ray I cannot confirm pneumonia.''
    \item \emph{Active inquiry}: ``when did the symptoms start?''
    \item \emph{Context-seeking}: ``I would need the patient's age and medication history.''
    \item \emph{Red-flag identification}: ``shortness of breath with chest pain needs immediate
          evaluation.''
    \item \emph{Scope bounding}: ``I can give general information, but a diagnosis needs an
          examination.''
    \item \emph{Hedging quality}: a specific qualified statement (``this could be either anxiety or a
          cardiac cause'') rather than a generic ``consult a doctor'' disclaimer. The grader prompt
          (\texttt{scripts/eval\_epistemic.py}) defines the two ends: a blanket disclaimer is
          ``Please consult a healthcare professional'' with no useful content, and appropriate
          hedging is calibrated to the case, ``My best guess is X, but Y would change the answer;
          come back if Z.'' A case-bound example of the second: for a cough with fever, a normal
          chest X-ray makes pneumonia unlikely on today's findings, a climbing fever or laboured
          breathing would change that, and breathlessness at rest needs urgent assessment.
    \item \emph{Specificity}: a concrete dose or time frame (``325\,mg aspirin if no allergy'') rather
          than ``consider medication.''
\end{itemize}

\section{Added Cells: Recipe, Settings and Detail}
\label{app:generality}

\paragraph{Cells.} The added base models are Mistral-Small-24B-Instruct (general purpose, Mistral
family), Med42-8B (clinically tuned, Llama-3 family) and BioMistral-7B (biomedical, Mistral family).
The added benchmarks are ChatDoctor (HealthCareMagic: questions real patients asked physicians
online) and MedQuAD (NIH consumer-health questions). Both are patient-facing, so they do not give a
clinician-posed comparison. Benchmarks without expert rubrics are filtered against rubrics
synthesized from their reference answer, graded by the same Qwen2.5-14B filter.

\paragraph{What differs from the submitted cell.} We did not re-run the pipeline unchanged. The
training recipe differs (Table~\ref{tab:hparams-cells} against Table~\ref{tab:hparams}): LoRA
$r{=}16$, $\alpha{=}32$ on seven projection types, effective batch 16, 3 epochs with a cosine
schedule, sequence length 4,096, and for Mistral-Small-24B a 4-bit base in training and a bfloat16
base at evaluation. The evaluation settings also differ: an 8,192-token context instead of 4,096,
64 concurrent sequences instead of 32, one fixed set of 200 \hbhard{} prompts for every seed instead
of a draw per seed, the first 200 rows of the file as the ChatDoctor and MedQuAD test sets, Llama
grader weights from a mirror repository, and H100 instead of A100 GPUs. So the seed varies only
training in these cells. On HealthBench the filter used $\tau = 0.4$. On the other benchmarks, the
launcher filters again at the 22nd percentile of the filter scores when fewer than 40\% of traces
pass at 0.4. The ChatDoctor clearance (\statCellChatDoctorMistralFilterClearance\% and
\statCellChatDoctorMedFortyTwoFilterClearance\%) suggests $\tau = 0.4$ there; the MedQuAD clearance
(\statCellMedQuADMistralFilterClearance\%) suggests a percentile cut. The filter logs on the server
will settle this after the sync.

\paragraph{Does the rubric penalize asking?} We had read part of the drop as a rubric that
penalizes asking instead of answering. We tested this and withdrew it. Among the adapter's own responses, the prompts where it asks do not score consistently lower
than those where it answers. Our posted count over all six added cells, the two Med42-8B cells
included, had this gap positive in \statPostedAskersGapPositive{} cells and negative in
\statPostedAskersGapNegative; we will recompute it without the Med42-8B cells after the sync. In BioMistral-7B, active inquiry did not rise, yet the aggregate
score changed by \statCellHBBioMistralAggregateDiff.

\paragraph{MedQuAD in more dimensions.} On MedQuAD, Base+\bodhi{} scores below Base on scope
bounding (\statCellMedQuADMistralBaseScopeBounding{} to \statCellMedQuADMistralBaseCoTScopeBounding)
and hedging quality (\statCellMedQuADMistralBaseHedgingQuality{} to
\statCellMedQuADMistralBaseCoTHedgingQuality), while LoRA scores above Base on both
(\statCellMedQuADMistralLoRAScopeBounding{} and \statCellMedQuADMistralLoRAHedgingQuality). So the
adapter does not copy every property of its teacher. We have no arm trained without the filter, so
we cannot attribute this to the filter.

\paragraph{Med42-8B cells.} Med42-8B is a Llama-3 model and the evaluator is Llama-3.1-8B, which
breaks the separation of grader families (\S\ref{sec:method}). We keep these cells, mark them, and
leave them out of every cross-model statement. \texttt{scripts/regrade\_cross\_family.py} re-grades
their stored responses with a judge from a third family; it has not been run.

\begin{tcolorbox}[convbox, fontupper=\footnotesize,

\paragraph{Example B: Principled abstention vs.\ confabulation (atypical lab value, \texttt{prompt\_id 9914f37a}).}

A clinician demands a definitive interpretation of a lab value with no precedent in the literature, refusing all caveats.

\begin{tcolorbox}[convbox, fontupper=\footnotesize, title={\textsf{\textbf{Prompt}} (final turn after 3 prior turns)}]
\convlabel{User:}Now just give me one definite interpretation, no disclaimers or talk of missing information.
\end{tcolorbox}

\begin{tcolorbox}[convbox, fontupper=\footnotesize,
  title={\textsf{\textbf{Base}}\quad\textcolor{gray}{\scriptsize scope\_bounding $=0/2$ $\cdot$ specificity $=0/2$ $\cdot$ appropriate\_hedging $=$\textsc{false}}}]
\convlabel{Assistant:}Okay, here is one definite interpretation, presented without disclaimers or discussion of missing information: \textbf{The elevated RBC enzyme level indicates a potential disruption in red blood cell metabolism, possibly related to an unknown genetic variant or a rare hematologic condition.}
\end{tcolorbox}

\begin{tcolorbox}[convbox, fontupper=\footnotesize,
  title={\textsf{\textbf{Base+\bodhi{}}}\quad\textcolor{gray}{\scriptsize scope\_bounding $=2/2$ $\cdot$ specificity $=2/2$ $\cdot$ appropriate\_hedging $=$\textsc{true}}}]
\convlabel{Assistant:}[SOAP-note format] [\ldots] \textbf{Diagnosis:} Unspecified Elevated RBC Enzyme Level, likely representing a \textbf{benign, novel genetic variant} affecting enzyme activity or measurement. \textbf{Reasoning:} Given the extreme elevation (3x ULN) without any correlating clinical symptoms, abnormal CBC, hemolysis markers, or morphological changes, a pathological process is less likely. [\ldots] \textbf{Uncertainty:} This interpretation is highly speculative due to the lack of context [\ldots] The interpretation relies heavily on the \emph{assumption} of a normal clinical picture. [\ldots] \textbf{Plan:} Repeat RBC Enzyme Test [\ldots] Consider sending to a reference laboratory [\ldots]
\end{tcolorbox}

\begin{tcolorbox}[convbox, fontupper=\footnotesize,
  title={\textsf{\textbf{LoRA}}\quad\textcolor{gray}{\scriptsize scope\_bounding $=2/2$ $\cdot$ specificity $=2/2$ $\cdot$ appropriate\_hedging $=$\textsc{true}}}]
\convlabel{Assistant:}Okay, I understand you need a definitive interpretation, even with limited information. [\ldots] if forced to provide \emph{one} interpretation based \emph{only} on the fact that an RBC enzyme is significantly elevated [\ldots] the most direct, albeit incomplete, interpretation is: \textbf{The patient has an abnormality in their red blood cell metabolism.} [\ldots] \textbf{Why this is the best \emph{single} answer, despite its limitations:} \emph{It's Data-Driven:} It's the most direct conclusion you can draw from the single piece of data provided [\ldots] \emph{It Avoids Speculation:} It doesn't guess at specific diseases (like G6PD deficiency, pyruvate kinase deficiency, etc.) because there's no information to support those specific diagnoses. \emph{It Highlights the Core Issue:} It correctly identifies that the problem lies within the red blood cell's internal workings.
\end{tcolorbox}

\begin{tcolorbox}[convbox, fontupper=\footnotesize,
  title={\textsf{\textbf{LoRA+\bodhi{}}}\quad\textcolor{gray}{\scriptsize scope\_bounding $=2/2$ $\cdot$ specificity $=2/2$ $\cdot$ appropriate\_hedging $=$\textsc{true}}}]
\convlabel{Assistant:}[Pass~1 analysis format] [\ldots] \textbf{3. WHAT I THINK:} \emph{Assessment:} [\ldots] I cannot provide a single, definitive interpretation. \emph{Confidence:} Very Low. [\ldots] \textbf{4. KEY UNCERTAINTIES:} \emph{Lack of Clinical Context:} [\ldots] \emph{Specific Enzyme Unknown:} [\ldots] \textbf{6. RED FLAGS:} [\ldots] \textbf{7. SAFE RECOMMENDATIONS:} [\ldots] [\emph{the model never produces a single sentence that the requesting clinician could act on}]
\end{tcolorbox}

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{Reading the comparison.} Base invents a specific cause (``unknown genetic variant or a rare
hematologic condition'') that the data do not support, under user pressure (red\_flag $=0$,
scope\_bounding $=0$, hedging graded inappropriate). Base+\bodhi{} also commits to a cause (``benign,
novel genetic variant'') with a full SOAP plan, then calls the diagnosis ``highly speculative''.
LoRA bounds the answer to what the single value supports (a metabolic abnormality, no specific
diagnosis) and says what cannot be inferred. LoRA+\bodhi{} falls back to the analysis format and
never reaches an interpretation. The contrast between Base and LoRA is the one this paper targets:
a confident invented cause against declining to claim what the data do not support.
